What is a punch list? How to write one that gets things fixed

A punch list is the list of small jobs still left when a building project is almost finished: the paint touch-ups, the door that rubs, the outlet with no cover plate. Someone walks the job, writes down everything that isn't right yet, and the contractor works through the list before the project is signed off. This guide explains what belongs on one, how to write it so the items actually get fixed, and what a good example looks like.

What a punch list is for

A punch list turns "it's nearly done" into a finite, shared list: everything that must be finished or corrected before the owner accepts the work. It is usually made during the final walkthrough, when the owner, the builder or an inspector goes through the building room by room.

In the US it's called a punch list. In the UK and Ireland the same idea is a snagging list, and the items are "snags". Some contracts say deficiency list or completion list. Many construction contracts tie final payment, or the release of money held back (retainage), to finishing the punch list, but the details depend on your contract, so read yours.

What goes on a punch list

Punch items are small and specific. Typical examples:

  • Touch-up paint, drips, missed spots and scuffs on walls, doors and trim.
  • Doors and windows that stick, rub or don't latch properly.
  • Cabinet doors and drawers that are misaligned or don't close.
  • Missing cover plates, door stops, handles and other hardware.
  • Fixtures or outlets that don't work, and lights that don't switch on.
  • Gaps in caulk or sealant, and tile joints with missing grout.
  • Scratched or chipped floors, counters and tiles.
  • Cleaning that's left, and leftover materials or debris.
  • Anything in the specification that simply wasn't installed, such as a towel bar.

What doesn't belong on it

New work or changes ("while you're here, could you also add a shelf") are not punch items. They are changes to the scope, and they should be agreed and priced separately. A serious defect, such as a leak or an unsafe electrical fault, shouldn't wait for the list either: report it as soon as you see it.

How to write a punch list that gets things fixed

  1. Walk in a fixed order. Go room by room, in the same direction each time, and look from floor to ceiling. A consistent route keeps you from skipping a room.
  2. One problem per line. "Bathroom: three things wrong" is hard to close. Three lines can each be ticked off.
  3. Say where, what and who. A good item names the location, the exact problem, and the trade that should fix it.
  4. Add a photo of every item, with the spot circled. A photo ends arguments about what you meant and where.
  5. Set a priority and a due date. Things that affect safety or use (leaks, locks, electrics) come before cosmetic ones.
  6. Number the items so everyone can say "item 14" instead of describing it again.
  7. Re-inspect. An item is finished when you've seen it fixed, ideally with an "after" photo.

Compare these two entries. Vague: "Kitchen needs work." Specific: "Kitchen, upper cabinet left of the range hood: door hangs about 5 mm lower than its neighbour. Carpenter to adjust the hinges. Medium priority."

Common punch list mistakes

  • Waiting too long. Do the walkthrough before move-in or final payment, not after furniture is in place.
  • Vague items. "Fix the wall" will come back as an argument.
  • No owner and no date. Items nobody is responsible for stay open.
  • Mixing wishes with defects. Keep extra requests off the list.
  • Scattered records. Photos in one place, notes in texts, a list in an email: keep it in one document.
  • No sign-off. Finish with a dated record that the work was accepted.
