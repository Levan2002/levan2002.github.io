New build snagging checklist: what to check, room by room

Snagging is the job of finding defects and unfinished work in a new-build home. Even good builders leave some: a door that catches, a patch of missing paint, a tap that drips. This guide gives you a room-by-room checklist, tells you what to bring, and shows how to turn what you find into a list the developer can't misread.

What snagging is, and when to do it

A "snag" is any defect or incomplete job in a new home. A snagging list is the list of them. Buyers usually snag when the home is nearly finished, often at the pre-completion viewing and again after moving in, because some problems only show up when the heating has run or a shower has been used. Ask your solicitor or the developer's sales team what you are allowed to do before completion and how defects are handled afterwards.

Many UK new builds come with a warranty such as NHBC's Buildmark, and builders are generally responsible for putting defects right during an initial period (commonly the first two years). Check your own warranty documents for the exact terms, and keep your snag list and all correspondence.

What to bring

  • A phone with a full battery, for photos and notes.
  • A torch, for dark corners, under sinks and the loft hatch.
  • A tape measure and a small spirit level.
  • A plug-in socket tester, which is cheap and shows wiring faults at a glance.
  • Masking tape or sticky dots to mark each defect, so the builder can find it.
  • Good daylight. Snag in the morning or on a bright day; low evening light hides scratches and uneven paint.

The checklist

Outside

  • Roof tiles or slates: none cracked, slipped or missing.
  • Gutters and downpipes: fixed firmly, no gaps, falling toward the drain.
  • Brickwork and render: no cracks wider than a hairline, no gaps in the mortar, no stains.
  • Paths, drive and garden: water runs away from the house, no pooling; fences and gates hang straight.
  • Outside taps, lights and drain covers: present and working.

Hall, stairs and landing

  • Front door: opens, closes and locks smoothly; check seals and the letterbox.
  • Stairs: no creaks, handrail firm, consistent step heights.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms fitted and responding to the test button.

Every room

  • Walls and ceilings: missed paint, nail pops, dents, and cracks. Note any crack you can fit a coin into.
  • Skirting and architraves: tight joints, no big gaps to the wall.
  • Doors: open and close, latch, and don't rub the frame or floor.
  • Windows: open, lock and close tightly; glass free of scratches; sealant neat.
  • Floors: even, no gaps, no springy or squeaking spots, no scratches.
  • Switches and sockets: test every socket, every light, and check the cover plates are fitted and straight.
  • Radiators: warm all over once the heating has run, no leaks at the valves.

Kitchen

  • Cabinet doors and drawers: aligned, even gaps, soft-close working.
  • Worktops: tight joints, no chips, sealant neat along the wall.
  • Sink and taps: run hot and cold, check the waste drains quickly, and look under the sink with a torch for drips.
  • Appliances: switch on every fitted appliance and the extractor.

Bathrooms and toilets

  • Run every tap and the shower; check hot water arrives and the drains clear quickly.
  • Flush toilets a few times and check around the base for leaks.
  • Tiles and grout: level, no cracks, no missing grout; sealant around the bath, shower and basin complete.
  • Extractor fan works and the door locks.

Before you leave

  • Take meter readings (gas, electricity, water) with a photo of each.
  • Count the keys and ask for manuals, guarantees and certificates for the installed systems.
  • Take a wide photo of every room, so you have a record of how it looked at the start.

A professional can check what you can't

You can find a lot with eyes and a torch, but some problems need tools: moisture readings, insulation checks, or a view of the roof void. If you want that, a professional snagging inspector can do the survey. Doing your own snagging first is still useful, because you know the house after living in it.

What to do with your snag list

  • Send it to the developer or the site's customer care team in writing, so there's a dated record.
  • Give each item a location, a description and a photo. Number them.
  • Ask for a date by which they will visit, and keep every reply.
  • Re-check each fix, and note the date it was done. Don't assume a repair has been completed correctly.

Common snagging mistakes

  • Rushing. Allow two to three hours for an average house, not twenty minutes.
  • Only looking at the finish. Run taps, flush toilets, open windows. Defects hide in use.
  • No photos. "There was a scratch on the worktop" is easy to dispute; a photo with the scratch circled is not.
  • Snagging once. New homes dry out and settle; some problems appear in the first months. Keep a running list.

Doing it with Siteward

Siteward has a snagging template built for this, with the rooms of a typical UK house already set up.

  1. On Projects tap New project and choose Snagging list ("New-build and handover snags"). It's at the top if your iPhone's region is the UK or Ireland; otherwise look under Formats from other countries.
  2. Enter a site name and the address, put yourself (or the developer) under Client, and add your name as inspector. Rooms & areas starts as Hall, Lounge, Kitchen, Bedroom 1, Bedroom 2, Bathroom, En-suite, WC, Landing & stairs, Garden and External. Edit the list to match your plot, then tap Create.
  3. Tap Add snag for each defect. Take photos with Camera, then tap a photo to circle the problem, draw an arrow, or add a dimension line (handy for a gap or an uneven door).
  4. Say it: "Kitchen, worktop chipped near the hob, high priority." Siteward fills the room, trade and priority; check them and tap Save & add next.
  5. Tap Report to build the Snagging Report as a PDF (in Options choose A4 or US Letter), then send it to the developer with Email or Share.
  6. When fixes are done, open each snag, add an After photo, and swipe it to Fixed. The next report shows before and after side by side.

Siteward is a note-taking and reporting tool, not a surveyor, so it can't tell you whether something meets building regulations or your warranty terms.
