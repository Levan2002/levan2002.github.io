How to moderate a Werewolf game: a narrator script and tips

The moderator, also called the narrator or game master, runs a Werewolf or Mafia game. They know everyone's role, wake the characters in the right order, announce what happened and keep the vote fair. Done well, it makes the game. This guide covers the order of the night, a simple script you can read aloud, the habits that keep a game fair, and the one big downside of moderating.

What the moderator does

  • Knows all the roles, and who holds each one.
  • Tells everyone when to close and open their eyes.
  • Wakes each role in turn and notes the choice they make.
  • Works out the result of the night: who dies, who is saved.
  • Announces the news at dawn, keeps time during the discussion and counts the vote.
  • Stays neutral. The moderator never hints, and never plays.

Before the game

  • Deal the roles face down and check that every player has looked at theirs without showing anyone.
  • Write down who is who, on paper, where nobody else can see it. You will need it every night.
  • Agree the house rules: does the first night have a kill, are roles revealed on death, how are tied votes handled, can the Doctor protect themselves.
  • Dim the lights and pick a calm voice. A slow, quiet delivery makes the night feel like a night.

The order of the night

Use the same order every night so it becomes a rhythm. A common order for the roles in a classic game is the Seer, then the Doctor, then the wolves. With more roles, the order Duskcaller uses is Cupid (first night only), the lovers, the Seer, the Sorcerer, the Doctor, the wolves and then the Witch. Any fixed order works, as long as you keep it.

A simple narrator script

You can read this almost word for word.

  1. Start the night. "Night falls over the village. Everyone, close your eyes and keep your hands still."
  2. Seer. "Seer, open your eyes. Choose one player." The Seer points. Give a thumbs-up if that player is a wolf and a thumbs-down if not. "Seer, close your eyes."
  3. Doctor. "Doctor, open your eyes. Choose one player to protect tonight." Note the choice. "Doctor, close your eyes."
  4. Wolves. "Werewolves, open your eyes. Silently agree on tonight's victim." The wolves point at one player. "Werewolves, close your eyes."
  5. Dawn. "The sun rises. Everyone, open your eyes." Announce: "Last night, the village lost Ben." Or, if the Doctor protected the victim: "The night passed quietly. Everyone is still here."
  6. Day. "Talk it over. Who among you hides a wolf's heart?" Start a timer of two to three minutes.
  7. Vote. "Time is up. It's time to vote." Count the votes. "The village has spoken. Cleo is eliminated." Reveal the role if you play that way.

Habits that keep it fair

  • Call every role every night, even if that player has been eliminated. Pause for a few seconds as if they were choosing. If you skip the dead Seer, everyone knows the Seer is gone.
  • Give the same time to every role. A quick wolf choice and a slow Doctor choice give the game away.
  • Cover the sound of pointing. Ask everyone to tap their knees softly while a role is awake, so nobody can hear who moves.
  • Keep your face still. Don't smile at a good guess or wince at a bad one.
  • Settle rules arguments quickly. Make a call, note it, and discuss it after the game.

Common moderator mistakes

  • Forgetting the Doctor's rule. Under the default rule they can't protect the same person twice in a row. Check the list.
  • Losing track of one-shot powers, like the Witch's potions or the Hunter's last shot.
  • Letting dead players coach. Remind eliminated players to stay silent.
  • Rushing the first night, when new players don't yet know what to do with their eyes.

The downside: the moderator doesn't get to play

The biggest cost of moderating is that you sit out. In a group of friends, someone always ends up as moderator all evening, or the group skips the game. A narrator app solves that: the phone does the job, so everyone plays.
