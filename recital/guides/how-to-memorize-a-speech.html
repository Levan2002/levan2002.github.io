How to memorize a speech

Whether it's a wedding toast, a talk at work or a class presentation, a speech is easier to learn than it looks if you work on it in the right order. This guide covers how much to memorize, how to break the speech up and how to practise so you can still deliver it when nerves kick in.

Decide how much to learn word for word

For a short speech, such as a toast or a two to three minute talk, learning every word is realistic. For something longer, learn the opening and the closing exactly, because those are the moments when nerves are worst, and know the middle by its structure and key phrases. It's fine to glance at a card in the middle.

Write it for the ear

A speech is heard, not read. Use short sentences, contractions and words you'd actually say. Read it aloud, and wherever you stumble, rewrite the sentence. Words that feel natural in your mouth are far easier to memorize than formal sentences you'd never say.

Learn the shape first

Before memorizing any wording, sum up each paragraph in a few words. If you can say the outline, you can find your place again after a stumble. Pay special attention to the transitions, the sentences that link one paragraph to the next. They're where most people dry up, so learn them on purpose.

Work one paragraph at a time

Learn the first paragraph until you can say it without looking. Then learn the second. Then say the first and second together. Add the third, and keep chaining. Each session starts from the beginning, so the opening gets the most repetition, which is exactly where you want to be strongest.

Say it out loud, on your feet

Reading silently doesn't prepare you to speak. Stand up and say the speech at the pace you'll use on the day. Record yourself once on your phone and listen back: you'll hear filler words, rushed sentences and places where a pause would help. If the speech has a time limit, time at least one full run-through.

Test yourself from memory

As soon as you know a paragraph roughly, stop reading it and start recalling it. Hide the text, say what you remember, then check. A card with just the first words of each sentence is a good middle step between the full text and nothing at all.

Space your practice

Start days ahead rather than the night before. Review the whole speech briefly each day, then do one or two run-throughs on the day itself. Twenty run-throughs on the morning of the event mostly add fatigue.

Plan for blanking out

Almost everyone loses their place at some point. Decide in advance what you'll do: pause, take a breath, repeat your last sentence, or jump to the first line of the next paragraph. A deliberate pause looks like confidence, and the audience usually doesn't notice anything went wrong. Keep a small card with the first few words of each paragraph in your pocket as a safety net.

Common mistakes

  • Writing it like an essay. Long sentences with several clauses are hard to say and hard to remember.
  • Never saying it aloud. Knowing a speech in your head and being able to speak it are different skills.
  • Memorizing it in only one place. Run through it once standing, in a different room, or in front of someone.
  • Racing. Nerves speed people up. Practise a slower pace than feels natural.

Doing it with Recital

Recital is an iPhone app for learning a text word for word, and a speech is exactly what it's made for. Everything runs on your iPhone, so a private toast stays private.

  1. Add your speech. Tap Add a text, then Type or paste (or Scan pages for a printed copy, or Import a file). Choose Speech under What is it? and tap Save.
  2. Open it and tap Listen to hear it read line by line, each word lit as it's spoken. It's a quick way to catch phrasing that doesn't sound right.
  3. Tap Fade. Words disappear step by step, the key words first. Tap Hide more until most of the text is hidden, and tap a gap to peek when you're stuck.
  4. Try First Letters, which leaves only the first letter of each word, and then Type Test, where you type each word's first letter from memory.
  5. Tap Recite Aloud, then the microphone, and say the speech from memory. Recital follows along on your iPhone and marks every word you skip or get wrong, and the results screen lists Words to work on. Tap the lightbulb for a hint if you freeze. Recite Aloud depends on your iPhone's on-device speech recognition for the language; if it isn't available for yours, Type Test and First Letters still work.
  6. Learn it paragraph by paragraph using the menu next to Practice, then finish with Whole text.
  7. Keep it fresh until the day. Recital schedules spaced reviews, and Daily review reminder in Settings brings you back when one is due. It doesn't time your speech, so time one run-through with your phone's stopwatch.
