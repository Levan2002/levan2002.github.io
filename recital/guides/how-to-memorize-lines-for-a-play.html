How to memorize lines for a play

Learning lines isn't about having a naturally good memory. It's about practising in a way that makes your brain work: understanding the scene, learning it in small pieces and testing yourself instead of rereading. Here's a method that works for a school play, community theatre or an audition.

Start by understanding the scene

Read the whole script first, not only your own lines. Work out what your character wants in each scene, who they're talking to and what has changed by the end. Lines you understand are much easier to remember than words that feel random, because every line follows from the one before it.

Then read your scenes aloud once or twice just to hear how they flow.

Learn in small pieces

Don't try to learn the whole part at once. Break each scene into beats: a speech, an exchange, a change of subject. Learn the first beat, then the second, then run the two together, then add the third. Short sessions repeated often beat one long session.

Learn your cues as well as your lines

Your cue is the last thing the other actor says before you speak. If you only know your own words, you'll freeze when the cue arrives a little differently. Read or hear the cue before each of your lines every time, so the two are linked in your memory.

Test yourself instead of rereading

Reading a page again and again feels productive, but mostly it makes the text feel familiar without making it stick. What makes a memory stick is trying to recall it. Cover your lines, say them from memory, then check what you missed.

  • Cover and reveal. Hide your lines with a sheet of paper and uncover one at a time.
  • Say them out loud. Speaking uses more of your memory than reading silently, and you'll find the awkward phrases early.
  • Use first letters. Write only the first letter of each word and recite from that. It's an old actor's trick because it prompts you without giving the line away.
  • Drill the same misses. You'll usually trip on the same few lines every time. Those are the ones that need extra work.

Tie the words to movement

If you know your blocking, rehearse the lines with the moves. Many actors find a line is attached to where they stand or when they pick up a prop, so it comes back on its own on the day.

Space it out

Cramming the night before works badly and is stressful. Go over the same scene on several different days: the next day, a couple of days later, then a week later. Sleep between sessions is a real part of how memory works. A scene learned two days before rehearsal and reviewed briefly will hold up far better than three hours the night before.

Run the scene with someone

At some point you need to say your lines to another voice. A friend or family member can read the other parts. If nobody's around, record the other characters' lines on your phone and play them back with gaps for yours. Running lines with the cues spoken aloud is the closest you can get to rehearsal on your own.

Common mistakes

  • Learning the sense but not the words. "Close enough" can throw off the actor who is waiting for your exact cue. Stick to the script unless the director says otherwise.
  • Fixing one way of saying a line. Learn the words plainly, so you can respond to what your scene partner actually gives you on the night.
  • Only practising silently. If you've never said it aloud, you haven't really rehearsed it.
  • Skipping the boring bits. The passages you find dull are the ones that fall out first.
  • Starting the week of the show. A little each day for a few weeks beats everything in a few nights.
