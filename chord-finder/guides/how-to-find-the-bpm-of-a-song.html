How to find the BPM of a song

BPM, or beats per minute, is the speed of a song. Knowing it helps you practice at the right tempo, set a metronome, mix tracks or cut a video to the beat. You don't need special gear to find it. Here are three ways, plus how to avoid the classic half-speed and double-speed mix-up.

What BPM means

A beat is the pulse you'd tap your foot to. BPM counts how many of them fit into one minute. 60 BPM is one beat per second, and 120 BPM is two per second, a pace many pop and dance tracks sit near. Slow ballads are often somewhere around 60 to 80 BPM, a lot of pop and rock falls between about 90 and 130, and faster dance styles go well beyond that. Those ranges are rough guides, not rules.

Method 1: count the beats

This needs nothing but a clock and a song.

  1. Start a 15-second timer on your phone and begin counting at the same moment.
  2. Tap your foot or finger along and count every beat. Count the steady pulse you'd naturally tap, not the fast hi-hat or the sparse kick drum.
  3. Multiply the number of beats by 4. If you counted 33 beats, the song is about 132 BPM.

For more accuracy, count for 30 seconds and multiply by 2, or count a full minute. A longer count smooths out small mistakes.

Method 2: tap tempo

A tap-tempo tool lets you tap a button in time with the music, then averages the gaps between your taps and shows the BPM. Many metronome and DJ apps include one. Tap at least eight times, and keep tapping until the number settles. The first few taps are usually a little off.

Method 3: let software measure it

Software can listen to a whole recording and measure the tempo more steadily than a quick tap. It works best on songs with a clear drum or rhythm track. It's less sure on live recordings, where the tempo drifts, and on free-time ballads that have no steady beat.

Half-time and double-time

The most common mistake is getting half or twice the right number. A song at 140 BPM with a snare hit on every second beat can feel like it's moving at 70, and both numbers describe the same music. Neither is exactly wrong, but one is more useful.

Decide by how you count the song. If you naturally count the bar as "1 2 3 4" at a comfortable walking pace, that's the tempo to use. If it feels frantic or sluggish, double or halve it. When you set a metronome to practice, use the number that makes it click on the beats you actually feel.

When the tempo isn't steady

Live drummers speed up and slow down, choruses can feel faster than verses, and some songs change tempo on purpose. For those, measure a short, steady section such as one verse, and treat the number as an approximation. For practice, that's usually all you need.

Why the BPM is useful

  • Practice. Learn a hard part at a slower tempo, then raise it step by step.
  • Metronome. Set your metronome to the song's BPM to play along in time.
  • Mixing and editing. Matching the tempo of two tracks, or cutting a video to the beat, starts with the number.
  • Running and workouts. Some people pick music whose tempo matches their pace.
