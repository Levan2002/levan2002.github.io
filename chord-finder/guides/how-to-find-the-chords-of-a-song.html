How to find the chords of a song

Maybe you heard a song you want to play, or you have a voice memo of an idea and can't remember what you played. Working out chords isn't magic. Most songs use only a handful of them, and a few habits make them much easier to spot. This guide shows how to do it by ear, what trips people up, and how to let your iPhone do the first pass.

Start with the key

Every song is built around a key, and the key tells you which chords are likely. A song in G major mostly uses chords made from the notes of the G major scale: G, Am, Bm, C, D and Em. In pop, rock and folk, a large share of songs lean on just four of those: G, C, D and Em. Once you know the key, you've cut hundreds of possible chords down to six. If you're not sure how to find it, read How to find the key of a song first.

Listen for the bass note

The lowest note of a chord is usually its root, the note the chord is named after. If the bass plays a G, the chord is probably some kind of G chord. Hum the lowest note you can hear, find it on a guitar string or a piano key, and you have the name of the chord. Do this at each point where the sound changes and you'll have the root of every chord in the song.

Not every bass note is a root. Sometimes a chord is played over a different bass note, which is written as a slash chord such as G/B. If a chord sounds right but a little unsettled under the bass, try the plain chord first and treat the slash version as a refinement.

Decide whether it's major or minor

With the root found, play it as a major chord and then as a minor chord and see which one fits. Major chords tend to sound bright and settled, and minor chords sound darker or more wistful. The key helps here. In G major, the chords built on E, A and B are minor (Em, Am, Bm), the ones built on G, C and D are major, and the one on F# is a rarely used diminished chord. Guess the in-key version first. If the song has a note that doesn't belong to the key, you may have found a borrowed chord, but that's the exception.

Find where the chords change

Tap your foot along with the song and count in groups of four. In most songs the chord changes on the first beat of a bar, and it often stays for one or two whole bars. Write the changes down as you go, like this: | G | G | C | D |.

Once you spot the loop of four or eight bars that makes up a verse, you'll usually find that the chorus uses the same few chords in a different order. Songs repeat themselves a lot, so working out one verse and one chorus gets you most of the way through.

Check your answer against the song

Play your chords along with the recording. If everything sounds right except one spot, change only that chord. The melody is a good clue: the note being sung when a chord arrives is usually one of the notes of that chord. If you can hum the melody while strumming your guess, clashes are easy to hear.

Slow the song down if you can. Hard passages are much easier at 70% speed, and many players loop a single bar until they're sure.

Common mistakes

  • Chasing fancy chords too early. Name each chord as plain major or minor first. You can add sevenths and suspended chords later, and getting the basic shape right matters more.
  • Trusting your guitar's tuning too much. Some recordings sit a little above or below standard pitch. If every chord sounds slightly off, re-tune to the recording or adjust your capo before you decide the chords are wrong.
  • Listening to the vocal only. The singing carries the melody, not the harmony. Listen to the bass, the piano or the rhythm guitar.
  • Ignoring the structure. The verse and the chorus often have different chord loops. Work them out one at a time.
  • Working from a muddy source. Heavy distortion, big reverb and crowded mixes hide chords. A cleaner version of the same song, if there is one, is easier to work from.
