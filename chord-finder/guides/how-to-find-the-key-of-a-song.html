How to find the key of a song

The key of a song tells you its home note and which chords belong with it. Knowing it helps you pick the right chords, sing in a comfortable range, choose a capo position and improvise a solo that doesn't clash. Here are three reliable ways to find the key by ear, plus a chart to check your guess against.

What a key is

A key is a set of seven notes, called a scale, with one note that feels like home. That note is the tonic. In the key of G major the tonic is G and the scale is G A B C D E F#. Most of a song's melody and chords come from those notes, and the song usually sounds finished when it lands on G.

A minor key works the same way with a darker scale. A minor uses the notes A B C D E F G, which are the same notes as C major, but with A as home.

Method 1: find the note that feels like home

Play the song and hum the note that feels most at rest, the one you'd end on if the music had to stop right now. That's usually the tonic. Find it on a piano or a guitar. Then play a major scale starting on that note and a minor scale starting on that note, and see which one sits better with the melody. This works well for simple tunes and gets easier with practice.

Method 2: match the chords to a key

If you've worked out the chords (see How to find the chords of a song), look for the key that contains all of them. Most songs lean on the I, IV, V and vi chords of their key. This chart shows them for common major keys:

A song built on G, C, D and Em fits the key of G major. A song built on Am, F, C and G fits C major, or A minor if Am sounds like home. When two keys share the same chords, the one whose chord feels like home is your answer.

Method 3: check the first and last chord

Many songs begin and end on the chord of their key. If a song finishes on a D chord, the key is probably D major, or D minor if the chord is minor. It isn't a guarantee, because some songs end on a different chord or simply fade out, but it's a quick way to get a first guess to test with the other two methods.

Major or minor?

Every major key has a relative minor key that uses exactly the same notes: C major and A minor, G major and E minor, D major and B minor, F major and D minor. To tell them apart, listen for which chord sounds like home. If the song settles on C, it's in C major. If it settles on Am, it's in A minor. Minor-key songs tend to sound more serious or wistful, but trust your ear for the resting point over the mood.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the first chord you hear. A song can open on a chord that isn't home. Check the ending and the chorus too.
  • Missing a key change. Some songs move up a half step or a whole step for the last chorus. Find the key of each part if the chords stop fitting.
  • Forgetting borrowed chords. One chord from outside the key doesn't change the key. Look at the majority of the chords.
  • Confusing a major key with its relative minor. They share notes, so the notes alone can't decide it. The resting chord can.
  • Taking the tuning for the key. If a recording is a little sharp or flat, the notes won't match your instrument exactly. Decide by the relationships between the notes, not by exact pitch.

Why the key is useful

A capo moves chord shapes into a new key without learning new ones, a singer can move a song to a range that suits them, and a guitarist or pianist can solo with the notes of the scale. If the key isn't comfortable for you, you can shift it up or down. Moving a song by one semitone (one fret) up or down changes every chord by the same amount.
