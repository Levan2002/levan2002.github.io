How to film your golf swing with a phone

Swing video is only as useful as the camera position behind it. A few minutes spent setting up properly saves you from chasing faults that are really just camera angles.

Published 6 October 2026

The two standard views

Most swing analysis uses one of two camera positions, and each shows things the other hides.

Face-on

The camera faces you, looking at your chest, with the lens pointing at a right angle to the target line. Line it up roughly opposite the ball and use the same spot each session.

Face-on is the view for things that move toward and away from the target: how much your head and hips shift during the swing, where the ball is in your stance, the shaft lean at impact, and how your weight moves into the finish. It is also a clear view for timing checkpoints, because the club's change of direction at the top is easy to see.

Down the line

The camera sits behind you, looking along the target line toward the target. Point it at your hands at address. Some coaches prefer to line the camera up with the hands, others with the toe line or the ball; any of these works, as long as you pick one and stick to it.

Down the line shows the swing plane, the path of the club and hands, your posture and spine angle, and whether you stand up or move toward the ball on the way through.

Height and distance

A good starting point for both views is:

  • Height: lens at about hip height. Waist or hand height also works; what matters is that it is level and the same every time.
  • Distance: about 3 metres (10 feet) from you. Close enough that you fill a good part of the frame, far enough that the club stays in shot at the top and at the finish.

Camera height changes how the swing looks. Down the line in particular, a phone lying on the ground or held up at head height shows the shaft and the swing plane at quite different angles from one placed at hip height, because you are looking up at the swing or down on it. Neither is wrong as a one-off, but if you mix heights between sessions, you will see changes that are not really there.

Framing

Your whole body and the whole club should be in the frame for the entire swing, including the top of the backswing and the high point of the finish. Leave a little room above your hands at the top and on both sides.

You do not need to turn the phone sideways. A golfer standing at address is taller than they are wide, so portrait framing fits the swing well. Hold the phone upright, keep it level, and make sure the horizon (or any straight vertical line, such as a door frame or pole) looks straight in the picture.

Use the main 1x lens rather than digital zoom or the ultra-wide lens. Digital zoom costs detail, and wide lenses stretch the edges of the picture, which can bend the shaft and distort angles.

Frame rate and light

A full swing takes about a second, and the part from the top to impact is a fraction of that. At normal video speed, the club can move a long way between frames and appear as a smear through impact. Slow motion fixes this:

  • 240 fps gives the most detail and the most accurate timing. It needs plenty of light, so it suits outdoor sessions.
  • 120 fps is a good all-round choice and copes better indoors.
  • 60 fps works when light is poor or your device does not offer more, but expect more blur and coarser timing.

Higher frame rates let less light into each frame, so a dim range bay or garage can turn grainy quickly. Face the light where you can: have the sun or the brightest light behind the camera, not behind you. A golfer standing in front of a bright sky becomes a silhouette, which hides the details you want to see. Some indoor lighting can also flicker on slow-motion video; if you see banding, try a different frame rate or a different light.

Keep it steady

A shaky or tilted camera makes everything harder to judge. Options, from best to worst:

  1. A small tripod with a phone clamp. Cheap, light and repeatable.
  2. A phone holder that clips onto an alignment stick pushed into the ground.
  3. The phone propped on your golf bag, a bench or a ball basket, wedged so it cannot tip.
  4. A friend holding it, as still as possible, with both hands and elbows tucked in.

Whatever you use, do not touch the phone during the swing. A countdown timer lets you start recording and walk back to the ball.

Common mistakes

  • Camera off-angle. A face-on camera that is not at a right angle to the target line, or a down-the-line camera aimed left or right of the target, distorts every line you draw. Lay a club on the ground along your target line to help line up the camera.
  • Too close. Lens distortion grows near the edges and the club leaves the frame. Step back.
  • Changing the setup every session. Different heights and distances make swings look different even when they are not. Mark your camera spot, or note the distance in steps.
  • Backlit golfer. Bright sky or a window behind you turns you into a shadow.
  • Body cut off. Missing feet or a club leaving the top of the frame makes checkpoints and body tracking less reliable.
  • Busy background. People walking past or a flag flapping in the background add motion that can confuse automatic analysis.

Safety first. Never stand in, or ask anyone to stand in, the path of the club or the ball. Place the phone where a mishit or a slipped grip cannot reach it, and check that the area is clear before each swing.
