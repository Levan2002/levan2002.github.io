How to measure golf swing tempo

Tempo is one of the few parts of a golf swing you can put a number on with nothing more than a phone. Here is what that number means and how to get it right.

Published 6 October 2026

What tempo is, and what it is not

"Tempo" gets used loosely in golf. For measuring, a narrower definition helps: tempo is the ratio between how long the backswing takes and how long the downswing takes.

The backswing runs from the moment the club starts moving away from the ball to the moment it changes direction at the top. The downswing runs from that change of direction to impact. Divide the first time by the second and you have a ratio.

tempo = backswing time ÷ downswing time

If your backswing takes 0.90 seconds and your downswing 0.30 seconds, your tempo is 3.0 : 1. Notice that the ratio says nothing about speed on its own. A player with a 1.05 second backswing and a 0.35 second downswing also has a 3.0 : 1 tempo, just with a slower overall swing. Total swing time is a separate figure, and worth noting alongside the ratio.

Why 3 : 1 comes up so often

If you read about tempo for long, you will see 3 : 1 quoted as a benchmark for full swings. The figure comes from people timing the swings of skilled players frame by frame, and many full swings from good players do land somewhere close to it. In practice you will find a spread, with plenty of good swings measuring somewhere between about 2.5 : 1 and 3.5 : 1.

It is better to treat 3 : 1 as a reference point than as a target you must hit. What tends to separate consistent players is less the exact ratio and more how little it varies from one swing to the next. A golfer who swings at 2.7 : 1 almost every time is usually in better shape than one who bounces between 2.2 and 3.6.

Where the ratio can be revealing is at the extremes. A very low number, say under 2 : 1, often goes with a backswing that is cut short or a rush from the top. A very high number can mean a slow, drawn-out backswing followed by a downswing that never gets going, or a long pause at the top. Both are worth a closer look.

How to measure it with slow-motion video

A phone that films in slow motion is all you need.

  1. Film at the highest frame rate you can. 240 frames per second is ideal outdoors; 120 is fine. The reason is precision: a downswing lasts only a fraction of a second, so the fewer frames you have, the more a single frame matters.
  2. Find the takeaway frame. Step through the video until the clubhead first moves away from the ball. Waggles and forward presses before the swing do not count; pick the frame where the actual backswing begins.
  3. Find the top. Step on until the club stops moving back and starts down. This is the trickiest frame to choose, because the hips and legs often start forward while the club is still finishing its backswing. Pick a rule, such as "the last frame before the clubhead reverses", and use the same rule every time.
  4. Find impact. The frame where the clubhead meets the ball, or the closest frame to it.
  5. Count and divide. Count the frames from takeaway to top, and from top to impact. Because both parts are filmed at the same frame rate, you can divide the frame counts directly without converting to seconds.

At 240 fps, the example swing above would be 216 frames back and 72 frames down: 216 ÷ 72 = 3.0. To get seconds, divide each count by the frame rate.

Why frame rate matters so much

Imagine the same swing filmed at an ordinary 30 frames per second. The 0.30 second downswing is now only about 9 frames. Be off by a single frame at the top or at impact and the downswing reads as 8 or 10 frames, which moves the ratio from 3.0 to somewhere between roughly 2.7 and 3.4. At 240 fps, the same one-frame slip barely changes the result. For tempo, more frames are worth more than more pixels.

What changes with wedges and putting

The ratio is mainly discussed for full swings. With shorter shots, the picture changes:

  • Full wedge shots are still full swings, and many players measure similar ratios to their longer clubs, sometimes a little lower because the backswing is often shorter.
  • Partial wedges and chips have short backswings and less time to build speed, so the ratio usually drops. Numbers closer to 2 : 1 are common.
  • Putting is often described with a ratio around 2 : 1 as well, with the backstroke taking about twice as long as the forward stroke to the ball. Putting strokes are gentle and short, so you need good slow-motion footage to measure them reliably.

Because of this, compare like with like. Track driver swings against other driver swings and 7-iron swings against other 7-iron swings rather than mixing them into one average.

How to use your number

  • Get a baseline first. Measure ten or so swings with one club before drawing any conclusions. A single swing tells you very little.
  • Look at the spread, not just the average. If your swings range widely, consistency is the first thing to work on.
  • Compare good shots with poor ones. Many golfers find their poor shots come with a noticeably quicker or slower ratio than their good ones. That pattern is more useful to you than any outside benchmark.
  • Change one thing at a time. If you try to adjust tempo, use a drill for a while and then measure again under the same conditions. The guide on golf tempo drills has some to try.
  • Keep it in context. Tempo is one ingredient of a swing, not a verdict on it. If a coach is working with you on something else, their advice comes first.
