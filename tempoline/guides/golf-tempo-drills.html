Golf tempo drills you can check on video

Good tempo feels unhurried, but feel is a poor judge of time. These drills give your swing a steadier rhythm, and a camera tells you whether it is working.

Published 6 October 2026

Before you start: get a baseline

Drills are easier to judge when you know where you started. Film ten normal swings with one club, say a 7-iron, from the same camera position, and note the tempo of each: backswing time divided by downswing time. Write down the average and the range from lowest to highest. If you have not measured tempo before, the guide on how to measure golf swing tempo explains how.

Look at the spread as much as the average. A wide spread usually means your rhythm changes from swing to swing, and that is often what the drills below help with most.

1. Counting

The simplest rhythm tool is your own voice. Pick a short count and say it out loud, or in your head, as you swing. A common one is to say "one, two" during the backswing, arriving at the top on "two", and "three" at impact. Because the backswing gets two beats and the downswing one, the count nudges you toward a smooth backswing and an unhurried start down.

Other players prefer words with a natural rhythm, such as "back and through". The words do not matter much. What matters is that the count is the same every time, so your swing has something steady to follow.

Try: ten practice swings with the count out loud, then ten balls with it in your head.

2. Three-tone trainer

A tone trainer replaces your count with sounds: one tone to start the takeaway, one where the club should reach the top, and one at impact. The gaps between tones set both the ratio and the overall speed. It takes the guesswork out of counting, since the beat cannot speed up when you are nervous.

  • Start with a slower setting than feels natural, for example a backswing of around one second at 3 : 1, and make practice swings without a ball until you arrive at the top and at impact on the tones.
  • Then hit balls with the tones playing. Expect the first few to feel slow; that is normal.
  • Once it feels comfortable, try shortening the backswing time while keeping the same ratio. Many players find a speed that feels both relaxed and powerful somewhere in the middle of the range.

Try a 2 : 1 setting for short chips and putting strokes, which tend to have shorter backswings relative to the forward stroke.

3. Feet together

Hit easy shots with your feet touching or only a few centimetres apart. With such a narrow base, a swing that rushes from the top or lurches with the upper body will throw you off balance, so the drill rewards a smooth transition and a swing where arms and body move together.

Use a short iron, tee the ball low, and swing at about half to three-quarter effort. Hold your finish for a count of three. If you cannot, the swing was probably too fast or out of sync. This drill is about rhythm and balance, not distance; the ball will not go as far, and that is fine.

4. Pause at the top

Swing to the top of your backswing, stop completely for about a second, then swing down and through. The pause removes momentum from the backswing, so you have to start the downswing deliberately. Many players who rush from the top find this drill shows them how much of their downswing was being started by the arms and hands.

Two cautions. First, your tempo ratio during this drill means nothing, because the pause is included in the timing, so do not measure it. Second, the goal is not to keep a pause in your real swing; it is to feel an unhurried change of direction. After a few paused swings, make a normal swing and try to keep that feeling.

5. Slow-motion rehearsals

Make full swings without a ball at a fraction of your normal speed, perhaps a third or a half, taking several seconds from start to finish. Keep the order of movement the same as your real swing: the club, hands, arms and body moving back together, then the lower body leading the way down.

Slow swings make it easy to notice where you tense up or lose balance. They are a good way to warm up, and a good way to rehearse a change your coach has given you before trying it at full speed. Finish with a few swings at three-quarter speed before going back to normal.

Checking your progress on video

Drills can feel like they are working when they are not, and the other way round. Video settles it.

  1. Keep the setup identical. Same club, same camera position, height and distance, same frame rate as your baseline. Otherwise you are comparing camera angles as much as swings.
  2. Film normal swings, not drill swings. After a drill session, hit ten ordinary shots and measure those. The point is how much of the drill carries over.
  3. Compare average and spread. A tighter range is real progress even if the average barely moves.
  4. Watch two swings side by side. Put a baseline swing next to a recent one, both starting from address. Differences in rhythm are often easier to see than to measure.
  5. Be patient. Rhythm changes take time to settle, and a single good session can be followed by a scrappy one. Look at trends over several weeks rather than day to day.

If a drill causes pain, stop. If you are working with a coach, check that a drill fits what you are working on together.
