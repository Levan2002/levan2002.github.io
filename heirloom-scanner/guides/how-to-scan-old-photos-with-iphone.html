How to scan old photos with your iPhone

You don't need a flatbed scanner to digitize old prints. A modern iPhone camera captures more than enough detail for a family photo, and scanning with a phone is far faster. What matters most is the setup: light, background and angle count for more than the camera. This guide covers what to do before you press the shutter, how to scan whole album pages, and how to bring faded color back.

Why the iPhone camera is enough

A 4×6 inch print that fills the frame of a 12-megapixel photo (about 4,000 pixels along the long side) works out to roughly 670 pixels per inch. That's far more than the 300 you need to print it again at the same size. What limits a phone scan isn't resolution. It's glare, shadows, a tilted phone and uneven light, which are all things you can control.

Get the setup right

  • Use soft, even light. Daylight from a window is ideal, as long as the sun isn't shining straight onto the print. Turn off the flash, and keep lamps from hanging directly over the print, because on glossy paper they reflect back into the lens.
  • Put the print on a dark, flat surface. A dark cloth or sheet of paper makes the edges of the print easy to see, for you and for software. Leave a little space between prints if you're shooting several.
  • Hold the phone parallel to the print. Tilting the phone turns a rectangle into a trapezoid. It can be straightened afterwards, but the less you have to correct, the sharper the result.
  • Fill the frame, with a small margin. Get close enough that the print takes up most of the picture, but leave a sliver of background so you don't cut off an edge.
  • Keep still and in focus. Rest your elbows on the table, tap the screen to focus, and keep your own shadow off the print.
  • Clean the print first. Brush off dust with a soft, dry brush or a microfiber cloth, and hold prints by the edges.

Scan albums without taking the photos out

Old prints often stick to magnetic or adhesive album pages, and pulling them off can tear them. If a photo doesn't come away easily, leave it where it is and scan the whole page. Open the album flat so the page doesn't curve. Plastic sleeves reflect more light than bare prints, so glare becomes the main problem. We cover that in How to remove glare when scanning photos.

Scanning a whole page also saves a lot of time: one picture captures several prints, and software can cut them apart afterwards.

Don't skip the back

The back of a print often holds the most valuable information: a date, a place, names. Photograph it too, or type what it says into the caption. Do it while you have the print in your hand. Handwriting fades, and the relatives who recognize the faces won't always be around to ask.

Fixing faded colors

Many color prints from the 1970s and 1980s turn yellow, magenta or pale with age. A little work on contrast and color balance brings most of that back. In the Photos app you can try Edit, then Auto, and adjust Contrast and Warmth by hand. The aim is to restore what the print used to look like, not to repaint it, so go easy and compare against the original print in your hand.

Common mistakes

  • Using the flash or a lamp directly overhead. Both put a bright reflection on glossy prints.
  • Shooting at an angle. It stretches the picture and softens one side.
  • Cropping too tight. Leave the border for now. You can trim it later, but you can't put back a cut-off edge.
  • Leaving every scan undated. They pile up as "IMG_4821" with today's date. Read How to change the date on scanned photos to avoid it.
  • Throwing away the originals or skipping a backup. Keep the prints, and back up your scans to iCloud or another service.

Doing it with Heirloom

Heirloom is built for exactly this job. It finds each print on the page, straightens it, restores the color and saves it with the right date.

  1. Open Heirloom and tap Scan Photos. At the top, choose Album page for several prints or Single photo for one. (No prints at hand? Tap Example to scan the included album page.)
  2. Lay the prints on a dark surface in soft light, or open the album flat, and tap the shutter button. You can also tap Import to use a photo you've already taken.
  3. Heirloom finds every print, up to eight on a page, and draws a frame around each. Drag a corner to adjust it, tap a frame to select it, or use the … menu to add or remove a frame. Tap Retake if the shot isn't good.
  4. If there's glare, tap Fix and take a second photo from a slightly different angle. The two shots are combined.
  5. Tap Continue. Each print opens on its own. Use Rotate if needed, Restore to bring back faded colors, and Trim to remove white borders.
  6. Set Date taken, or tap Read the back to photograph the writing on the back of the print. Add a Caption if you like.
  7. Tap Save Photo (or Save 3 Photos for a page of prints). The photos go into your Heirloom library, which groups scans by decade. Unless you turn off Also save to Photos in Settings, they also go into the Photos app, dated when they were taken.
