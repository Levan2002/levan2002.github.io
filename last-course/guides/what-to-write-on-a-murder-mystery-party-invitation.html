What to write on a murder mystery party invitation

A murder mystery invitation has more to do than a normal party invitation. It has to get people to turn up, tell each guest who they will play, and keep a few secrets safe until the night. Here is what to put in it, how to word it, and what trips hosts up.

The five things every invitation needs

1. The practical details

Date, start time, address and roughly when the evening ends. Murder mystery parties run longer than a typical dinner, so tell people up front. A case that plays for about two and a half hours plus drinks on arrival is a three-hour commitment, and guests with babysitters will thank you for saying so.

Say whether dinner is served, and ask about allergies and dietary needs in the same message. Add an RSVP date, a few days before the party at the latest, because everything else (the cast, the seating, the menu) depends on the final headcount.

2. A character for each guest

This is what makes it a murder mystery invitation rather than a dinner invitation. Give every guest a name, a role and a short public description: the one-paragraph version everyone at the table is allowed to know. “Marguerite Vale, the restaurant critic whose reviews can close a kitchen” is enough to start a conversation before anyone has said a word.

3. What to wear

Guests want to know how far to go. Give a dress code in one line, then two or three costume suggestions made from things people already own. A short list works better than a theme name alone, because “1920s” means a full flapper dress to one guest and a waistcoat to another.

4. A private brief, kept separate

The secrets belong on their own page, sent only to the guest they belong to. A good private brief covers what the character is hiding, what they want, what they know about the others, and what they will say if asked where they were. Label it clearly (“for your eyes only”) so nobody forwards it by accident.

5. One sentence on the rules

Something like: “Stay in character, guard your secret, and please don’t compare briefs before the night.” It sets the tone and heads off the guest who wants to show their partner everything.

A sample you can adapt

You are invited to a murder.

Saturday 17 October, 7:00 pm, at 14 Alder Street. Dinner is served, and the evening should wrap up around 10:30.

Dear Sam, you will attend as Dr. Edmund Pryce, the family physician. Everyone knows he has treated half the village for thirty years. Not everyone knows why he left the city.

What to wear: a tweed jacket, a stethoscope or a doctor’s bag, reading glasses on a chain.

Your secret brief is attached on a separate page. Read it alone and tell no one what it says. Please reply by Wednesday with any dietary needs.

That is about a hundred words and it does everything above. Note the one thing it deliberately leaves out: the solution. An invitation should hint at intrigue without revealing who did it, or why.

When to send it

One to two weeks ahead is the sweet spot. Less than a week and guests can’t find a costume; more than three weeks and the date slips their mind. A short reminder the day before, with the address and the start time, is welcome and costs you nothing.

Mistakes that cause trouble

  • Posting every brief in a group chat. Send each guest only their own pages, in a one-to-one message. Once a secret is in a shared thread, the game is over before it starts.
  • Revealing too much in the public description. If it says “secretly in debt”, it is not public. Keep the public line to what the whole room already knows.
  • Forgetting the end time. A game that quietly runs an hour over is a bad surprise for anyone with an early morning.
  • Giving a shy guest a heavy role. Match the character to the person. Quieter friends often do best with a character who has a secret to guard rather than a speech to give.
  • A vague dress code. “Dress up” produces a very mixed table. Name the era or the setting and give two examples.
